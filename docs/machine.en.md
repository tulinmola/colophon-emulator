A machine assembled from parts can be honest part by part, and these are. A chip whose account of itself is incomplete says so at the head of its own header, because a part that quietly does nothing is worse than a part that is missing: the missing part is a question, and the quiet one is an answer that happens to be wrong. This page collects those declarations in one place, for a reader who is not going to open the headers. A line leaves it the day it stops being true.

## The machines

Four names answer, and a name is listed only once the machine behind it boots its own firmware to its own prompt.

| Name | Machine |
| --- | --- |
| `cpc6128` | Amstrad CPC 6128, 128K, BASIC 1.1 |
| `cpc664` | Amstrad CPC 664, 64K, BASIC 1.1 |
| `cpc464` | Amstrad CPC 464, 64K, BASIC 1.0 |
| `spectrum48` | Sinclair ZX Spectrum 48K |

The three Amstrads differ in the firmware they are given and the memory they are built with, which is very nearly how they differed on a desk. A 64K machine has no PAL fitted, so a banking command written to it falls into an empty socket and nothing moves — which is what happened on the hardware, and what a program written for 128K discovers there.

## The processor

The Z80 is complete and cycle-stepped: one call advances it by a single clock cycle, and every instruction the machine knows is implemented, the undocumented ones included. The register model is the full one — the shadow set, the index registers as halves that act as registers in their own right under a prefix, and WZ, the internal address latch the datasheet never admitted to.

The NMOS quirks are there, because both machines were given an NMOS part and programs can see them. `OUT (C),0` puts zero on the bus. `SCF` and `CCF` take their two undocumented flag bits from the accumulator or from the old flags depending on whether the instruction before them wrote flags at all. An interrupt landing on `LD A,I` or `LD A,R` leaves the parity flag reporting interrupts disabled when they were enabled — a bug Zilog acknowledged in 1989 and fixed on CMOS.

Interrupt modes 1 and 2 are implemented. Mode 0 executes whatever a device puts on the data bus, and on neither of these machines does anything drive it, so the processor reads &FF: an `RST &38`, which is mode 1's behaviour. That case is implemented and the general one is not, because neither machine carries the hardware to reach it.

## The Amstrad CPC

Six chips and a PAL, and the accounting below is of them.

### Memory

The memory map is the board's: sixteen kilobytes of lower ROM over the bottom of the address space, an upper ROM over the top, and the eight RAM configurations the 6128's PAL selects between. The upper ROM is chosen by a number latched at &DFxx, and a number with no ROM in its socket resolves to ROM 0, which is BASIC — as it does on the hardware, and which is why an unfitted expansion answers with something rather than nothing.

I/O is decoded the way the board decodes it, by single address bits rather than by whole port numbers. One access can therefore reach several devices at once, and a program that relies on it is not doing anything unusual. An access the Gate Array's wait line holds over several ticks reaches each device once.

### Video

The 6845 is emulated as **type 0**, the HD6845S/UM6845 — its register widths, the counters a program can leave above their limits and the widths that bring them home, the frame construction, the two video pointers and their reload rules, and the line that ends the frame being decided while the character counter is still at 0 or 1, so that a register written after that cannot take the decision back. A register written at the line's second character is the exception, and it lands on the third: there it can unmake a last line and spend it on padding instead, or make one of an ordinary line and bring the row counter home under it.

Interlace is here, deadline and all. An interlaced picture is two fields drawn half a line apart, and the machinery that offsets them is present: the chip keeps the two frame parities the documentation gives it, adds to every even frame the one line that makes the fields differ in length, and holds that frame's frame sync back to the middle of its line. In the interlaced video mode it counts differently as well — the raster address becomes the scanline counter doubled, with a parity filling the bit that leaves, so a character row covers twice the ground and the two frames address alternate lines; the register that sets a row's height is read up to that same parity. Which parity is the frame's own, except where a row is an odd number of lines, when it turns about with the row's number as well, so that rows come out alternately long and short and a pair of them still holds what a single row held before.

Writing that mode moves two things at different moments: the doubling waits for the head of the next line, while the parity joins the limit at once. That is what makes a row entered or left off its parity overrun — it counts the whole way round five bits before it finds its end again — and a program uses exactly that to discover which of the two frames it is on. Shaker times a frame in that mode at 20032 microseconds, which is what a real machine times.

The parity the frames alternate in has no defined start: silicon wakes holding whatever it holds, and this chip wakes on the parity its type gives that first frame. Every frame after inherits that choice, so a program that reads the parity rather than setting it can be a frame out here and right on a real machine. The frame sync's own permission, described below, has no defined start either, nor has the permission that manages the counters, and this chip wakes holding both, which is the state a chip that has been running is in.

The line interlace adds to an even frame is asked for later than the adjustment lines: the question is put on the last line the frame has, at the character its width register names as that line's last, and the line it is put on may be one of the adjustment lines itself — so a program can add the line, or take it back, from inside an adjustment, long after the window for asking for adjustment lines has shut. The suite's group for it grades that reach into an adjustment on the third machine below: were the question put only on the last line of a frame's last row, never on an adjustment line, two more of the lines it grades there would come out wrong. It bounds the character too. Put at the line's forty-second character, where one of its tests writes the register that asks for interlace on a last line, or at any character before it, the question puts at least one more line wrong; from the forty-third character to the last, the group cannot tell one from another. The last is the documentation's word.

Five CRTC types shipped in real machines and they diverge observably; demos probe for the type at runtime and branch. Only type 0 behaves as itself here, with eight exceptions. The first is what a program can read of the chip, which is what the probing reads. Every one of the five answers it now. Three of them hand back a table of registers, one of those three having a status register besides; the two built around an ASIC read through a table of eight instead, chosen by the bottom three bits of the number a program asks for, so that several numbers name the same register — and two of those eight are not registers at all but bytes tracing where the chip's counters stand, which a program can read to wait for an event to the microsecond. Their two ports answer alike. One bit of those two bytes is left standing where it rests rather than tracing anything: it counts lines from the frame sync, and the documentation describes it twice in terms that cannot both be true of the same resting value, so a program reading it here learns nothing. Until those two answered at all, the suite could not tell them from the third type: it named both of them that, ran that type's tests on them, graded them against that type's expectations, and left five of its groups — including the two written for these chips — sitting at their menus. It names them correctly now, with one reservation that is ours rather than the suite's. The two ASICs are told apart chiefly by the computer around them rather than by the chip: the earlier of the two lives only in a machine with a further ASIC, an unlock sequence and a deliberately imperfect copy of the keyboard chip, and this emulator has none of those. The documentation also believes one bit of the second status byte differs between the two chips, but says so tentatively and does not say what the difference is, so nothing here can act on it. So a machine built as that type is a chip in a computer that was never sold, and the suite reads it as the later one. That is the right answer to the question it actually asks. The second exception is the syncs. A line sync asked for with a width of nothing is none at all on types 0 and 1, and sixteen characters on the other three, which is one of the ways a program tells them apart; no line the suite grades moves on it. A width written while a line sync is already running is taken here as a count to reach: one the count has yet to reach ends the sync where the count meets it, and one it has reached or passed, zero among them, runs the sync on until its counter comes round. For a width already passed the documentation agrees on every type, save a zero on a type 1, which ends that sync outright. Three such writes have outcomes of their own in the documentation, and none of the three is here. One is that type 1 zero. Another is the width the count stands at, written on that very character: from the instruction that lands its write on its third microsecond behind a Gate Array — one of the two that can make such a write — it stops the sync on types 0, 1 and 2 (the trick the documentation calls R3.JIT), where here the sync runs on round the counter, as it does on the two ASICs, which the trick does not work on. The last is a zero landing inside the sync's first microsecond: it cuts the sync short on types 0 and 1, or never starts it if the other of the two instructions wrote the zero; here that sync too runs on to sixteen. Four groups of the suite are aimed at these writes, and none of them grades itself in words. The two types that cannot ask for a frame sync's length hold it at sixteen lines whatever the register says, and a sync either of them is made to begin by writing the register that triggers it gives up the rest of the line it began in, where type 0 keeps it. Type 0 also holds a sync back by a whole line on alternate frames where the interlaced video mode leaves a row an odd number of lines, and neither of those two does, which is one of the things that leaves such a machine's two fields out of step with each other. No line the suite grades moves on either of those two: what stands behind them is the documentation's own sentences and tests of our own. One more divergence of the frame sync stands beside those two, and this one the suite does grade. A program can make the comparison that raises it come true by hand, writing the register that names the row with the row the machine is already on; at the head of a line that blocks the sync rather than raising it, leaving the machine with neither the sync nor the comparison until a later change of either register brings it round. The documentation gives that blocking to type 0 in type 0's own chapter and gives a type 1 no exception at all; the third machine it gives the sync at once as well, except while a line sync is running and on the character it ends on, where it gives one that counts its lines but never reaches the pin, and it gives the same pinless sync to the comparison the row counter walks into while a line sync runs. So here a type 1 and the third machine take the sync where type 0 is blocked, and the third machine's is pinless wherever its line sync is running or has just ended: it counts its sixteen lines and holds another sync off as any frame sync does, and the Gate Array sees nothing of it — no frame on the monitor, no interrupt count set back, no blanking — while the 8255 reads the pin low. For the comparison the row counter walks into, a line sync that only begins on that character does not count, as the documentation has it. One group of the suite bears the pinless sync out: on a line it grades, the machine matches silicon's value. Another sets the line sync where the documentation says the third machine can raise no frame sync at all, and the machine now raises none; the frame sync that group then tries to make through the 8255's port B is not wired here. A frame sync held back to the middle of its line on an even field takes the pinless sync by the same reading where the line sync runs into or ends on that middle character, which the documentation does not describe and nothing grades. The two built around an ASIC keep type 0's block, for no reason their own documentation gives and with nothing graded on it, and refuse the sync by a rule of their own besides, which is here: a sync starts on those only where the row named is reached with both the row's line counter and the character counter at zero, so a comparison made by hand anywhere but that corner is spent, and the sync it asked for waits for the corner of the frame after. Where the row named is the frame's own first, that corner is also the character the field turns over on, and those two take them in the other order from the rest: they read the sync first and turn the field after, where the other three turn the field and then read the sync. So on those two a sync named for the first row is held back to the middle of its line on the odd field of an interlaced picture rather than the even one. Two groups of the suite grade that on each of their records, one of them timing an interlaced frame padded out by extra lines and the other putting a frame sync against an interlaced frame, and both came right on it. The documentation has that comparison made at the corner and only the start held back, by half a line or by a whole one; this machine makes it where the start falls instead. So a register written to meet the row inside the row's first line, after its corner, still raises the held-back sync here where the documentation says it raises none, and a comparison true at the corner and undone before the start — the register rewritten, or the interlaced mode given up, which leaves that frame no sync at all — loses a sync the documentation raises. Nothing grades any of it. What the chapter that gives them the corner denies those two beside it is not here — it says they have no protection against a comparison raising a second sync, where this machine protects every type. Nothing on any record moves on the corner or on the protection: the group that watches this very pin to catch a row counter overrunning came right on both records by two other rules, set out below, and neither the corner nor the protection touched one of its lines. The eighth exception below reaches the line of the suite that grades it, on the second and third machines' records alike, by their early reading of the comparison, with or without the block. One of the two things that keep a single comparison from raising two syncs is missing here: the documentation forbids a write of the triggering register from serving the same row again while a sync is already running, and here any such write brings the comparison round whenever it is made. The other protection, that the comparison itself must have changed, is kept. The third exception is how a type 1 divides a row between the two fields of an interlaced picture: it leaves a row an odd number of lines from an even setting of the register that sets a row's height, where type 0 does so from an odd setting, and it reads that register down to the row's own parity where type 0 reads it up. So the two machines want different settings of that register for rows of the same height, and a picture built for one comes out of the other with its rows a line adrift. This is the second of the five the suite grades, and it is what took the second machine's record from a quarter of what it can score to two fifths. The third machine that can be named here shares none of it: its interlace is a different arrangement again, and most of that is now here. Its rows are not halved in the interlaced video mode as every other machine's are — a row keeps the height its register asks for, so eight lines stay eight — and what steps by two instead is a display counter of its own, which shows each address twice. That counter is also where the machine leaves the address a row hands the next one: twice over a row of an even number of lines, and once over a row of an odd number, where the row ends a line before the counter comes round and the second is lost. The parity that counter is paired with is the field's own and nothing besides, where the other four machines balance a pair of rows between their two fields: a row's addresses here do not alternate as a row's do on those. One of the arrangement's rules is still missing: the mode is taken up at the head of the line after the one asking for it, as on type 0, where the documentation has this machine take it the moment it is asked and change the address under a line already being drawn. Two groups of the suite are aimed at it and neither grades itself yet. The other is here. A mode switched on during a frame's own first line, on the odd field of the pair, turns that line into one of the lines a frame adds: the frame begins again beneath it, both counters back at nothing, and runs a line longer. The documentation offers that line to a program as the way to learn which field it is on, and the suite takes it. Before several of its groups it switches the mode on at a frame's head, looks for the extra line, and turns the field over once if it is not the one it wants. Without the line that look found the same answer on either field, so those groups were graded on whichever field this machine happened to wake on. With it the group that puts a frame sync against an interlaced frame agrees in all eight of its lines, where four were wrong; the group that times an interlaced frame padded out by extra lines came right; the two wrong lines of the group that asks for the interlace line with a write on a frame's last lines came right; and every line the suite grades comes out the same whichever field the machine wakes on, which was measured rather than assumed, though some of the pictures its ungraded groups draw still move with it. A fourth group, aimed at this very line and saying its piece in a picture, now holds that picture still where before it rolled without end. The documentation's own table for switching that mode on draws no such line, where three passages of its prose describe it; the prose is followed, as the suite follows it: its routine for settling the field works only on a machine that draws the line. Where the mode is given up again inside that first line, the counting goes on as it would have: the documentation says that of the line a frame's end adds, and reading it as reaching this one is ours. Of the line a frame's end adds it is not here, and the frame ends after that line whatever the register has become. The group that asks for the interlace line with a write on a frame's last lines turns the mode off on that line on the third machine, and two other lines it grades there are wrong for want of it: the machine sends the row counter home on the next line, where silicon keeps counting. The fourth exception is the parity the two fields alternate in. Type 0 decides the coming frame's parity in advance, on a row a register of its own names, so a program that puts that row beyond the row counter's reach freezes it — the frames stop alternating, the two fields stop differing, and the extra line falls on every frame instead of every other. A type 1 decides nothing in advance: it turns the parity over at the head of every frame whatever the register that asks for interlace holds, and no register can freeze it. What a program is given instead is a pulse of the interlaced mode, on and off across an even scanline, which settles the parity even whichever it was; that is here too, taken on the two microseconds of the writing instruction the documentation puts it on. This is the third of the five the suite grades, and it took the record from three eighths to three fifths: both groups came right and fell silent. The pulse moves the row's own line counter with it, and a program can see that too: in the interlaced mode the low bit of that counter is the parity rather than the count, so a pulse laid on an odd line of an even frame puts the counter back to the line before and the frame runs a line longer than its neighbour. A third group grades that, thirteen times over, and all thirteen of its lines are right now where six were wrong. What the counter costs is the second half of it. The documentation gives a type 1 one counter, which is the address itself and steps by two through an interlaced frame with the parity filling the bit it leaves; this machine keeps a count and a parity apart, which is the arrangement the documentation gives type 0, so wherever a mode is asked for or given up in the middle of a row the two have to be handed back to each other. The thirteenth of those tests is what measures it: its pulse spans a scanline, so the doubling starts between the test's two writes. The rules behind the pulse are graded by sixteen worked scenarios the documentation draws for them, each named for the test that exercises it and each drawing that counter beside the two parities, and all sixteen are checked here. The two machines built around an ASIC have a rule of the same family and it is here: a write that turns the interlaced video mode on sets their line parity from the line it lands on, without the correction a type 1 makes for its row height. Taking it on that write alone is ours: the documentation's words cover a write that asks for either of the two interlace modes, and nothing a program can see tells the readings apart, and in that mode their line counter is the address as a type 1's is, so the count and the parity are handed back to each other at the mode's edges the same way. What they keep that a type 1 does not is a counter the write itself never moves. No line on any record reaches any of it: what stands behind it is the documentation's own algorithm for those two, the two frames it works out by hand, two of the twenty-two counting cases it draws beside them, and tests of ours. One limit stands with it. The line an interlaced frame adds is not given these two as the documentation gives it: it leaves their row counter where it is, and the line itself carries no parity — the documentation is explicit that the line's own scanline "will always be 0, even if the other lines are odd" — and neither is here. Nothing on either record grades that now: the group that did has drawn nothing past its menu since the machine began naming itself, which the suite records as run and kept rather than skipped. Where a row's line counter is sent past its own limit, these two bring it home: they do not test that limit for equality at all but for its having been reached or passed, which the documentation says twice and says is what makes overflowing impossible on them. The group that checks the two registers behind it agrees on both records, and so do two lines of the group that puts a frame sync against an interlaced frame. They also decide a frame's end where the line ends rather than at its head, from the register naming the last row and the one setting a row's height as the two then stand. A last row's register written under the row counter there sends the counter on past it, which the documentation calls an overflow; written with the row the counter stands on during that row's last line, it ends the frame on the next line, whatever character the write landed on, and earlier in the row the frame ends where the row does; and the row height written at or under the line a frame's last row has reached ends the frame on the next line too. What type 0's own chapters give it there instead — a last line unmade near its head spent on padding — is type 0's and not these two's. The group that watches the frame sync to catch the row counter overrunning grades exactly that, twelve readings on each of the two machines, and agrees with every one of them now — which the records show only by the group falling silent, as it prints nothing where it agrees: this rule and the microsecond described below together, without which the later of its two writes arrived a microsecond early and overflowed where silicon does not. The earlier of the two chips has an exception of its own there, taking such a write before the counter comes home when the processor is selecting a ROM in the same instant; it is not here, and the suite, which names both chips the later one, does not ask for it. The padding after a frame is asked for at the same moment, from the register that sets it as it then stands and from the interlaced mode as it stood on the line's last character, where type 0 asks early in the line and holds the answer: so these two pad a frame whose padding was asked for anywhere in its last line, and a line of two characters, too narrow for type 0's own asking, takes no padding for its narrowness on these two, whose documentation says their line width "accepts all values without causing any problems for other counters". A line of one character still freezes them, as it freezes every machine here, and still pads their frame as it pads type 0's; and the padding that type 0 gives a row the counter has already carried past the last one is theirs here too, their chapters saying nothing of it. The second and third machines are given type 0's early asking, where their documentation, like these two's, has the register read at every character. The pointer a row hands the next one is left alone through the lines that pad a frame out on these two, where every other machine moves it on the first of them: the documentation gives that padding to them "without updating the video pointer". The register that counts those lines is read the same way as the row's own limit on these two, so a program that drops it below the line already reached ends the padding there and then. Type 0 and the third machine test it for equality instead and run on until the counter has climbed its whole round back to it, which is thirty-two lines; the second holds the frame open altogether, as described below. The fifth exception is where the lines that pad a frame out to its full height are counted. Type 0 has no counter of its own for them and spends the row's own line counter, so the row stands still while they run; two of the others do have one, and go on counting the row beneath them, so the row counter advances through the padding. A picture built for one comes out of the other with its last rows differently numbered. No line the suite grades moves on the counter itself: what stands behind that is the documentation's own tables and a test of our own. Only one of those two machines latches a state when it opens the padding, and a program that then cancels the padding does not clear it: the frame stays open, and the program closes it on whatever line it likes by asking for the padding again. It comes to an end by itself as well: the row counter goes on being compared with the register that ends a frame, and on the row where it comes back round to it the counter returns to zero while the padding runs on, until the counter that measures the padding comes round in its turn. That group is the fourth of the five the suite grades, and it now agrees with silicon on all eight of the lines it states, where five of them were wrong before. What is still missing with the counter is the single line a line too narrow to be taken back would otherwise give. The sixth exception is the border asked for by the register that names a screen's last row, when it is set to zero. On type 0 and one other a frame's first line comes out a chequer of bordered and displayed bytes, and a program can still take the setting back — until the character where the line's own width is reached, after which the border stands for the rest of the frame. A type 1 borders outright on that setting, wanting no row to match it, and gives the border up the moment the setting changes, unless the change was made while the counter still stood on the frame's first row, which keeps it for that frame. The remaining two have no conflict at all and read the setting where a line begins rather than through it, so one written mid-line waits for the next. No line the suite grades moves on any of the three: what stands behind them is the documentation's own chapters and tests of our own. The seventh is where a type 1 takes the address a screen begins at. The other four read it once, at the corner where all three counters stand together at zero, and keep it in the latch a row reloads from; a type 1 takes it into the pointer itself, and takes it afresh at the head of every line for as long as the frame's first row is running. A program written for another machine that sets the next screen's address early, while that row is still on, has it taken at once — which the documentation names as the reason a 1987 game loses the scenery behind its score on that chip. The documentation keeps that taking alive past that row in two further cases, and one of them is here: where a frame's first row runs into the extra lines a frame can be padded with, the taking carries into the first row of that padding and no further — unless the register that ends a frame was moved above zero at the very last character of a line, which takes it away. The other, where a missed border leaves the taking alive whatever row the chip is on, is not. The eighth is how wide a window a write has to reach the comparisons a character clock settles. None of these machines has quite finished deciding a line's end on the clock that took it: a write finishing on that same clock still moves the width under the comparison already made, so the line that had ended did not end and the counter goes on from the character it stood on. A type 1 holds that window open across the character after it as well — its diagram runs to five placements where the others run to four, and the documentation names the reason a phase shift inside the chip. A character was drawn in that second microsecond and it counts: the counter goes on from what was drawn and not from the ending, or the line comes out a microsecond too long. On a line a single character wide that second microsecond is itself the next line's end, and a type 1's write reaches past it to the end before — its diagram draws that very line, a line of nothing given a width, with the write from one of the two instructions that can make it going on a character further on a type 1 than on types 0 and 2 — so the chip keeps the last two line ends rather than one. A write finishing on that same clock reaches another comparison too, the one that starts the frame sync, which a type 1 reads on the character the write lands on where type 0 and the two ASICs read it on the character after. The third machine reads it there too, on the suite's evidence alone: its documentation sets it beside type 0 in that phase and says only that such a sync is triggered at once. What that buys a program is the last chance the documentation grants such a write — up to the final microsecond before the comparison comes true — one microsecond later than type 0 and the two ASICs have it, the row counter having already walked onto the value being written, where those three do not read that equality until the character after, by which time the block described above has spent it. The board is what says which edge an access landed on, and a board that cannot say leaves every machine here without the window its line ending is kept in, this one without the second character besides, and it and the third machine without the early frame-sync reading. What the chip cannot take back is the character it has already drawn — its address and its syncs went to the video hardware before the write arrived — so a machine spends one character of a line that turned out not to have ended, or two where a type 1's write reached the second. It is spent twice over. Undoing what the ending did is not the same as making the comparisons the character would have made had the line run on, and all but two of those are lost. One is the row counter's permission to count, which a line too narrow to reach its second character is refused, and which the line that runs on is given back. The other is a type 1's alone: the character it drew since the end still meets the new width, so where that width names the character the end was taken on, the line ends there and the character drawn since begins the next. Two of the things the lost comparisons cost fall later in the line rather than on it. The frame sync's permission, described below, is raised at a particular character, and a character that never asks for it costs the following line its frame sync — on a line of two characters, which is the width the trick is used at, that loses a sync the chip raises. And where a line ends on the character just before the one the display's width register names, the character the counter goes on to is the one a longer line would have met that register on — so the rest of the line stands displayed, where a machine whose line was that long all along has already gone to border. The suite grades the window at both its widths and not that character, and the lines it came right on are three of the six a group prints for this exact trick, and on the second machine's record a fourth of the six, where the line is a single character wide. Two things about that width are ours rather than the documentation's, and both follow a recording from silicon against a diagram. The second character is one placement past what the diagram draws, the last row of each of its three diagrams showing the line wrapping where the suite says it runs on. And the window is reached only by a write the board reports finishing on the character clock, which, on the three machines behind a Gate Array, one of the two instructions that can make such a write does and the other does not — so there they stand one microsecond apart where the documentation sets them two, the second instruction never being rescued where the first is. What the documentation is really about, one machine reaching a placement beyond the rest, is kept. Both readings are what carry three of that group's lines on the records of the first three machines, while leaving every frame of the demo records where it stood; the later ASIC's record grades that trick too and agrees in all three, its program counting from an interrupt that comes a microsecond later on that machine, as described below. The two machines built around an ASIC take one of the two instructions that can make such a write a microsecond later still than the other three: the documentation puts that instruction's write on the third microsecond for a chip sitting behind a Gate Array and the fourth for an ASIC emulating a chip, and that microsecond is here. The board holds such a write for the next character clock and hands it over there, which is where it hands over the other instruction's write on every machine. Behind an ASIC both instructions therefore reach the window, and their last placements in time stand the one microsecond apart the documentation draws for those two, where behind a Gate Array they stand a microsecond closer than it draws them. The documentation's line-ending chronograms fix the microsecond each write lands in, the last in time of each on a line's last character, and nothing finer; taking both on the clock is this board's own choice, and it agrees with the single microsecond those chronograms put between the two instructions' last placements. The documentation's drawing of how an ASIC samples the bus names no instant inside the microsecond. A read of one of the chip's write ports, which writes it, is still taken where it falls on those two. Of the status register only the border bit is answered; the bit beside it reports a light pen, and no board here wires that pin, so it and the two registers the pen would fill stay empty. Where silicon gives one of two bytes for a register it never defined, this gives the larger of them, and nothing outside this repository grades the choice. The behaviour behind the name is what the rest of this work is.

Two more of that register's bits govern the border, and both of the things they ask for are here. The display can be handed back a character or two late at each end of a line — the delay falls on the signal as it leaves, not on the comparisons that raise it, so a program that takes the delay off after the border it deferred keeps the rest of its line — or it can be shut outright for as long as those bits stand. Shutting it silences the interlace bits below as well, on a sentence the documentation itself marks as wanting further investigation. Two more answers are ours rather than the documentation's: shutting the display does not cancel a border the row register has already raised — the documentation asks that question and leaves it open — and the byte-by-byte alternation described below follows the delayed signal rather than the undelayed one. Nothing outside this repository grades any of it: the suite that tests these functions draws pictures we can read but cannot mark, so what stands behind them is our reading of the documentation and nothing sterner. The firmware's own screen asks for none of this; it is demos that reach for it. Two bits above them delay the cursor the same way, and the cursor is not here at all.

The extra lines a frame can end with are set up on every last line and taken back at the third character if nothing wants them, which is the order the chip works in. A line of two characters cannot reach that character and keeps what it was set up for; a line of one keeps it too, where the stopping falls on the character the line begins with — the single row advance the stopping still lets through is the run beginning rather than a row ending, and it carries the setting-up past the very last line that same advance unmakes. A run already under way is past cancelling, however the registers move afterwards: it ends where the count of lines says and not before. So a run can begin under a stopped picture and outlive it: the row holds where the stopping left it, and when the line is widened again the scanline goes on being counted against the run's own length until it is done. The single line the documentation grants such a narrow line is drawn here too, and the order of the asking is what tells the two cases apart: a line too narrow to be taken back is entered and then measured, so it draws one line and ends on the next, while a wider one is measured first and may draw none at all. That is not a nicety — built the other way for every line alike, the machine answered four of the places the suite grades a line too long, where it had been right in all seven. What the narrow line itself shows, nothing outside this repository has measured.

The cursor and the light pen will stay missing: no board wires their pins, so nothing they do ever leaves the chip. Not implemented in the 6845: those two, and some of the states this type settles at the head of a line and acts on later. Three are here: whether the frame's last line is followed by extra ones is settled where the chip settles it, at the third character of the line rather than the first, so that a program is obeyed whether it asks for those lines in time or takes them back. A register moved at the second character can also turn that last line into an extra one itself, and moved there the other way it makes a last line of a line that was not one, the row counter coming home under it, which is the chip's own way of adding a line without asking for any. The second is the frame sync's own permission, raised at the third character of every line and spent at the head of the next: a line too short to reach that character costs the following line its frame sync, and spends the comparison that would have raised it, so widening the line again does not bring it back. A program that shortens a line to two characters — which demos do, building a screen out of lines a microsecond or two wide — loses a frame sync it never meant to, and one that sets the sync register to the row it is already on gets a frame sync only if the line has passed its second character; at the head of a line it gets the block instead. The third is the permission the counters themselves are given, withdrawn at the head of every line and returned at its second character: a line of a single character never reaches that character, so nothing is managed again, and the row, the scanline within it and the frame sync's own count of lines all hold where they stand — which is why a frame sync begun there never ends. What the last managed line had already decided still lands, and a row advance is all of it that can. The documentation gives that standstill to type 0 alone and says of the others that a width of nothing causes their counters no trouble at all, three of them in as many words; here all five stand still. Taking the standstill away from the other four was built and measured, and it is not what they want: a demo recorded on a type 1 lost 866 frames of its run, a group of the suite whose whole business is settling a machine on a line of one character stopped settling, and the one line of the suite that grades exactly this came out no better. So the standstill stays, and what the other four want in its place is not simply its absence. One thing about that width is settled, and it is ours rather than the documentation's: where a width of nothing arrives under a counter that has already left the head of its line, this machine puts the counter home at once instead of leaving it to climb its eight bits and come round. The documentation describes a width of nothing only from a counter already there, so what stands behind the rest of it is one group of the suite, which grades it on four of the five types and says nothing of the earlier ASIC — agreeing on all four. The line sync's width is counted in characters rather than lines and goes on counting, so a line narrow enough to sit inside it ends before its own sync does. Every other comparison is made afresh at each character, which agrees wherever no register moves mid-line and parts company the moment one does. The width of the display is also judged the moment it is written, as the documentation asks, so a program that sets it to nothing at the head of a line borders that line; the one character the write arrives on keeps the picture it already had, where the documentation borders that one too. Two of the border's rules move the display enable *inside* a character. One puts a byte of border at the end of any line whose width register was never reached, unless a delay is programmed, which carries that border to a whole character of its own instead. The other alternates shown and bordered bytes along a frame's first line, where the row register is zero. The chip is still stepped once per character; what it reports for each of them is that signal as each of the character's two bytes finds it, which is as fine as those two rules ever ask for.

A line shorter than its own line sync cannot run two of them back to back: the second is refused, so the syncs come every other line rather than in one unbroken stretch — which is what stops this type falling into the endless sync the others do, and it matters because that pin is what the Gate Array counts its interrupts on. A program can have the second after all by writing the sync's width on the character the first ends on, whatever value it writes there, and then the new sync carries the old count on instead of starting from nothing. Two things about that second sync are not here. The chip drops the line for a fraction of a character between the two, which the Gate Array sees and draws as a sync of its own, where this pin is reported once a character and can only stay high; and a width written there that differs from the old one is read a character early, by the comparison that ends the first sync as well as by the one that starts the second.

The Gate Array holds the colour registers, the ROM enables, the video mode, and the interrupt generator: the six-bit counter of line syncs that raises the interrupt every 52 lines, a microsecond after the end of the line sync that asked for it — and the same microsecond on the interrupt the frame's own check raises, which the documentation does not say either way — the request line held until the processor acknowledges, bit 5 killed at the acknowledge, and the rule that, two line syncs after a frame sync begins, the counter is cleared — raising an interrupt on the way only if bit 5 was set. On the two machines built around an ASIC the Gate Array is handed both of the CRTC's syncs a character late, their documentation having the line sync begin "at the start of the display" of the character the CRTC began it on, and the frame sync going with it so that the two arrive in the order they left: every interrupt there comes a microsecond later than on the other three, the black painted over each sync and a change of mode waiting for the line sync's end come a character later with them, and the picture sits a character further left, as the documentation says of the later ASIC plugged into the monitor of one of the other three; the monitor here is set up once for all five, where the one sold with the later ASIC's machine was set to centre its frame. What a program reads of the frame sync is still the chip's own, undelayed. The counter acts on a line sync a microsecond after it ends, on the second of the four processor cycles that microsecond is made of, and it hears an acknowledge where the processor's acknowledge cycle ends, which is always on that second cycle: one microsecond on from a cycle begun in the first half of a microsecond, two from one begun in the second. Which of the two comes first is the whole of a race the documentation describes. An acknowledge heard before the count that gives the counter bit 5 takes away a bit the counter has not yet gained, so the counter keeps it and the next interrupt can come twenty lines later instead of fifty-two; heard after, the bit is there to be taken and the full fifty-two stand. Which of the two a program gets is settled by the length of the instruction it let the interrupt through on, and it is the one place the processor's own timing reaches inside the interrupt generator. An instruction whose last cycle falls before that second cycle does not see an interrupt raised in its final microsecond and runs one more instruction first, as the documentation describes the race and as the suite finds it on the machines whose line syncs do not come out of an ASIC — the two built around an ASIC disagree on record, catching the interrupt one instruction sooner. The character their syncs arrive late by does not account for it: a delay of whole microseconds moves every interrupt alike, and the group counts from the interrupt itself. What it asks is where inside a microsecond those two raise the interrupt, which their documentation does not say. Nothing finer than a quarter of a microsecond is kept, where the documentation times that race to a sixteenth and finds the type 1 parts differing from one another on that scale; a program that sits on the boundary gets the answer a type 0 gives. Taking an interrupt costs five microseconds after a one-microsecond instruction, as the documentation measures it, and the instruction that does the same thing from code costs the four it gives. It turns each pair of fetched bytes into sixteen colour samples in whichever of the four modes is in force, and a mode change takes effect after the next line sync rather than at once. Its palette is the one measured on the outputs of a real 40010, so the colours are the ones a machine produced rather than the ones its logic implies.

Not implemented: the 40010's habit of starting mode 2 one pixel early. It waits for Shaker.

The monitor is a cathode ray tube and knows nothing about computers. It receives colour samples and one composite sync line, and separates line retrace from frame retrace by how long the sync is held — which is what a tube does, and the reason a program that trims a sync pulse short moves the picture sideways: a line is timed from the middle of its pulse, so a shorter pulse walks that middle earlier and the picture half a character to the right.

Not modelled: the flywheel a real tube runs its horizontal oscillator on. Every sync edge retraces here, and a frame that outruns the tube clamps at the bottom of the screen where a real monitor would lose vertical hold and roll.

### Timing

The machine runs at the right speed, and the reason is the Gate Array rather than the processor. It holds the CPU off the memory for three cycles in four so that the video fetch always wins, which rounds every machine cycle up to a whole microsecond and costs the processor a quarter of its nominal 4MHz. That tax is what makes a CPC a CPC: it is why a program that counts instructions to reach a raster line reaches it.

The chip knows nothing about which cycle the processor is in. It generates the same pattern continually, and the CPU meets it wherever its own sampling happens to fall — which is how instructions whose lengths do not divide by four end up linearised onto the microsecond anyway.

### Sound and the keyboard

The keyboard is the real matrix: ten lines of eight switches, read the long way round — the processor asks the 8255, which asks the sound chip, which reads the grid. Both joysticks are there, including the one that shares its line with the letters, which is why its directions can be played from the keyboard.

Not modelled: keyboard clash. On hardware, three keys held at the corners of a rectangle in the matrix conjure the fourth, because the switches are a grid of wires with nothing to stop a current going the long way round.

The 8255 implements mode 0 — plain input and output — which is the only mode any program on these machines selects. A port turned to input presents &FF to whatever is wired to it, which is not a detail: it is what the device on the other side reads while the processor is reading, and getting it wrong stays invisible until something depends on it.

The AY-3-8912 keeps its registers and its one port, and **makes no sound**. The tone channels, the noise generator, the mixer and the envelope are stored and not sounded. The keyboard is read through the chip regardless, which is why typing at the prompt works with no audio anywhere in the machine.

### The cassette

The deck is the same one a Spectrum has, wired to the pins a CPC brings it to: bit 7 of the 8255's port B is what is at the play head, and bit 4 of port C is the motor. That motor is the difference between the two machines. A CPC starts and stops the tape itself, so a program may take as long as it likes between blocks; a Spectrum has no such line and must keep up with a reel that never waits.

What the machine writes to tape — port C's bit 5 — goes nowhere. Nothing here records.

The format is the `.cdt`, which is a `.tzx` under another name, so one reader serves both machines and refuses the same blocks. A `.tap` is not one of them: it is bytes at the Spectrum ROM's own timings, which no CPC firmware can read, so it is refused here rather than played into a machine that will never make sense of it. No CPC tape has been loaded through the firmware here: the wiring is graded, the loading is not.

### Snapshots

A machine writes itself out as an SNA snapshot and another reads it back and carries on. Versions 1, 2 and 3 are read, taking the fields they share; version 1 is written, because every emulator can read it.

What version 1 cannot carry is the CRTC's internal counters, so a machine resumed from one restarts its frame instead of continuing mid-raster. Version 3 has room for them, along with the CRTC type and the drive's motor and head — worth reading the day there is something here that can act on them.

### Discs

A disc is three things here, and each is its own part: the medium, the drive that turns it, and the controller that reads it.

The medium is a sectored floppy as an IBM System 34 controller finds it. Both DSK layouts are read: the original, which gives every track one length and every sector the same allotment, and the extended one, which gives each track a length of its own and each sector the length it truly occupies. That second one is what lets an image describe a disc that lies — a sector announcing a size it does not hold, an identity with nothing recorded behind it, a data field that reads differently on each revolution — and none of the disagreements between what a sector claims and what is true are corrected, because protected discs are built out of exactly those disagreements. A size code of eight or more announces 32K, which is how the chip itself counts; the image definition's older rule that only three bits counted was retracted by its own author, and Arnold's table measured on a chip agrees. An image that records where its sectors lay, in the Offset-Info block SAMdisk writes, is believed; one that does not has its sectors laid out where a formatter would have put them, gaps and all, with the gaps shortened evenly when the revolution has no room for them. Every byte of a track then has a position, so that a controller told to read past a data field finds the check, the gap and the next sector's identity — which is what some protections read past the end to look for. A disc can be written in place: a data field rewritten, a track formatted again into the room its image already gives it. An image written back is written in the extended layout, with the sector positions, and reads back as the same disc.

A track formatted larger than it was takes fresh room past the image's end, as far as the host said its buffer reaches; the command line gives every disc room to format each track once more. Not modelled in the medium: a track with more than the 29 sectors a header describes, a data field written where the image recorded none, and a format with no room left anywhere — it is reported as a write-protected disc, which is the nearest thing the software can show. A track recorded at any rate or in any mode but the one these machines read holds nothing a head can find, and keeps its place and its bytes so that an image written back still carries it.

The drive owns the motor, the head, the side and the turning. A revolution is 200ms at 300 rpm and a byte passes every 32µs, so a sector is found when its identity comes round and not before. Drive A is the machine's own one-headed 3" drive and B is the connector for a two-headed second; the motor port turns both, as the board's does. Not modelled: how long a motor takes to reach speed, which no source measures — the operating system waits a full second — so READY comes with the motor and the host may set a delay the day someone measures one; and the mechanical stop a head hits past its last cylinder, so a head steps as far as the medium is wide and finds unformatted tracks there.

The controller is a µPD765A, ticked in microseconds. Every command in its set is implemented — Read Data and Read Deleted Data, Write Data and Write Deleted Data, Read a Track, Read ID, Format a Track, the three Scans, Seek, Recalibrate with its limit of 77 steps, Sense Interrupt Status, Sense Drive Status and Specify — and every one of them is graded by a test except Scan Low or Equal and Scan High or Equal, which share Scan Equal's path and differ only in the comparison. With them come the three phases and the RQM, DIO, EXM and CB handshake the operating system polls, since neither the interrupt nor the DMA lines are wired on these boards. The data moves one byte every 32µs and a processor that has not collected one in time has overrun. Every field is judged by the bytes that pass: the chip runs its check over the marks, the identity or the data and the two check bytes, and reads the data mark for itself, so a sector a formatter wrote at one length under an identity announcing another fails its check here as it does on the disc. A command in FM finds nothing on these discs, which are MFM. The drives are polled for a change of READY between commands, at the datasheet's interval doubled for the 4MHz clock, as every other timing is; a check on that doubling is that AMSDOS programs a 6ms step rate in the datasheet's units and documents it as 12. Terminal count is a pin the machine never raises, so a read that reaches the last sector it was asked for steps past it and ends with the end-of-cylinder flag set — which is what every read on a CPC looks like, and what AMSDOS tests for.

Not modelled in the controller: DMA mode, which the boards cannot use, is taken as non-DMA mode except that EXM stays low; the microseconds the chip takes between one command byte and the next RQM, and between the end of execution and the first result byte; and the Version command, which this revision of the part does not have. The Scans compare sector by sector but their result is an approximation of the datasheet's table.

On the CPC the interface answers at A10 and A7 low — the motor at A8 low, the controller's two registers at A8 high — and is built into the 664 and 6128; a 464 gets it plugged in, with the AMSDOS ROM as upper ROM 7, the day a disc is given to it. That an access held by the wait line reaches a device once, as the memory section says, matters here more than anywhere: this chip hands over its next byte on every read.

## The ZX Spectrum

A Spectrum is one chip where a CPC is two. The Ferranti ULA counts out the frame, reads the screen, turns bytes into pixels, raises the interrupt, holds the border colour and gates the keyboard, and the board around it is little more than sixteen kilobytes of ROM, some RAM and eight address lines running to forty keys. What that buys is a machine with almost no wiring to get wrong; what it costs is that everything interesting happens inside the one part.

It boots its firmware, shows the copyright message Sinclair put in it, and answers arithmetic typed at the keyboard. The picture is read back off the beam and not out of the display file, so the serialiser, the composite sync and the tube are all in the path that is checked.

The screen and the processor share one bank of memory, and the ULA settles that argument by stopping the processor's clock rather than by asserting a wait line — so a held T-state here is one the processor does not run, while the beam runs on without it. What it costs depends entirely on when: an instruction that reads and writes the screen can take twice as long as the book says, and the same instruction begun a few T-states later costs nothing at all. That is why a Spectrum program's speed is a property of where in the frame it began, and why one that needs its timing to hold waits for the interrupt before it begins.

The interrupt itself is a window rather than an event. The ULA holds the line low for the first thirty-two T-states of a frame and no longer, and the processor looks at it only on the last T-state of an instruction — so an instruction that ends on the thirty-second takes it and one that ends a T-state later waits a whole frame. Nothing on this board drives the data bus while the acknowledge is on it, so what the processor reads is the floating &FF; in mode 2 that is what makes a Spectrum's vector table 257 bytes long and read at I:&FF.

A memory access is charged once, at the T-state it begins. An internal T-state that only holds an address is charged like an access of its own, because these ULAs weigh the address and not the request. A port is charged by a rule of its own, at up to four of the T-states of the one access, turning on the port's low bit as well as its address. An interrupt acknowledge is charged nothing, and nothing here can settle whether the chip would: the acknowledge always falls in the top border, where nothing is owed under any rule.

Three things are missing.

**The EAR socket hears nothing the machine itself writes.** On hardware the EAR and MIC sockets and the speaker share one ULA pin, so a program that writes to bit 4 of port &FE changes what bit 6 reads back, by a path whose exact behaviour is what separates an issue 2 board from an issue 3. Here the socket hears the deck and nothing else. Loading is unaffected — the tape drives the pin — but the handful of programs that measure the machine by writing and reading their own bit will not find what they wrote.

**The floating bus is not modelled.** The chip fetches ahead of the beam through a pipeline that this collapses into a single T-state, and that pipeline is exactly what a read of an unattached port observes. Software that steers by it — waiting for the beam to reach a particular place by watching what the bus happens to be carrying — will not find what it is looking for.

**Nothing sounds.** The speaker bit is stored and never heard.

A tape plays, in both the formats a Spectrum's came in. A `.tap` is bytes at the ROM's own timings; a `.tzx` records the timings themselves, block by block, which is what a tape that brought a loader of its own needs — and most commercial releases brought one, for speed and to be hard to copy. Either way the deck plays the edges and the firmware measures the time between them, exactly as it did on hardware. A Spectrum has no motor line, so the tape turns from the moment it starts and the machine cannot stop it, which is why loading was always a race the program had to win.

What a `.tzx` can still ask for and not get is a waveform recorded sample by sample, data built from a table of symbols, or blocks visited out of order by a jump, a loop, a call or a menu. So is a block whose identifying byte the reader does not know, because the format gives a block's length nowhere but its own table and an unknown one cannot be stepped over. All of those are refused when the tape goes in rather than played wrongly, and a refusal is the whole tape and not one block. So is an image that does not hold together — one whose blocks run off its end, or whose last byte of data claims to carry more than eight bits — because a tape read past its own bytes is worse than a tape not read.

What is not refused is an element of no length: a pulse of no T-states, or a run of none. Rippers leave those in the tails of blocks and distributed tapes carry them, so they are passed over in silence, as they sound.

What the machine writes to tape goes nowhere here, as on the CPC: bit 3 of a write to port &FE is the MIC socket, and it is stored and goes no further. Nothing here records.

A tape can also stop before it ends: a multiload marks the point between its parts, and there the reel stops where a real one would have. Playing it again carries on from the next block — but nothing here presses PLAY a second time, so from the command line a multiload loads its first part and waits.

A snapshot it keeps too: the 48K `.sna`, read and written. What that format has no room for is where the beam stood, so a machine picked up from one begins its frame again from the top — the same limitation the CPC's snapshots have, for the same reason.

One smaller declaration. The colours are chosen rather than measured: the shape of the answer is fixed by the hardware, one line per gun and one more for brightness, but no measurement of what those lines reach was found, so the levels are the ones the field has settled on and a real reading would replace them.

## The firmware

The firmware images are Amstrad's — the CPC's are Locomotive Software's as well — and they are fetched rather than committed — distributable with emulators under the permission Amstrad gave in 1999, which is the ground every emulator in this field stands on. The machine is built without them and cannot boot without them.
